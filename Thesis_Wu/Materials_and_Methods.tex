\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}
The dataset provided by \cite{makin2018} is available at \href{https://zenodo.org/record/583331#.Xv2qFygzaUk}{Nonhuman Primate Reaching with Multichannel Sensorimotor Cortex Electrophysiology}.
It containes the recordings from two Rhesus macaque monkeys, named Indy and Loco.
Each monkey performed a number of sessions, and in our research, a session means a consecutive experiment recording within several minutes.
The lengths of the sessions are not consistant, but normally they are in the range of 5 to 20 minutes.
Two 96-channel silicon microelectrode arrays were chronically implanted into their sensorimotor cortex.
Each array consisted of 96 electrodes, and the distance between two adjacent electrodes is $400 \mu m$. All electrodes covered the area of size $4mm \times 4mm$.
For each monkey two arrays were implanted, one was in primary motor cortex (M1) and the other was in primary somatosensory cortex (S1).
Some sessions of the dataset were made with the recording from both arrays, whereas most of the sessions only consisted of the recording from the M1 array.
The sampling rate of both arrays is 24.4kHz.

The monkeys were trained to perform reaching tasks with their arms in a transverse plane, with targets showing up in a virtual reality environment.
Circular targets in 5mm virual radius were presented in Figure \ref{fig:panels} which the size is either $8 \times 8 $ square grid or $8 \times 17$ rectangular grid for monkey Indy,
and for monky Loco square grid of size $6 \times 6$ was used.

\section{Data Preprocessing}
The electrode arrays' sampling rate was 24.4kHz, as mentioned in Section \ref{sec:dataset}, and their recordings were filtered with an IIR bandpass filter (fourth-order Butterworth; $F_{pass}$=500Hz to 5000Hz).
Spikes were detected and sorted online by \cite{makin2018} with a custom software written in C++.
In Figure \ref{fig:raw_to_spike}, the upper subplot is the raw broadband signal and the bottom subplot is the filtered high-frequency signal and the detected spikes.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{raw_to_spike.png}
        \caption{Raw Data Waveform and the Detected Spikes}
        \fontsize{10}{12}
        \label{fig:raw_to_spike}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

Due to the spike detecting threshold they assigned in different sessions, the sorted units in each session varied from 2 to 4.
For all sessions preserved an unsorted \textit{hash} unit for each recording channel.
Theses units contained all events which surpass the threshold on a channel but which did not match any of the operator defined templates.
However, \cite{todorova2014sort} showed that there exists some information in the hash unit thus keeping them is benefitial in the movement decoding tasks. 
As showed in Figure \ref{fig:indy_20160407_02_M1_ch_60}, which visualized the M1 spike train in channel 60 from session \textbf{indy\_20160407\_02}.
The black rasters are the spikes classified as hash unit, and this session has two more sorted units, marked in red and blue respectively.
Figure \ref{fig:indy_20160407_02_S1_ch_60} is the spike train from S1 electrode array in the same session and same time interval.

\section{Deep Neural Network}
\subsection{Recurrent Neural Network}
In our study, we used the model name Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) as neural decoder.
The structure in each cell is showed in Figure \ref{fig:GRU_cell}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{GRU_cell.png}
        \caption{Inside of a GRU Cell\\ \colorbox{green}{這是網路上允許再利用的圖，有CC4.0 licence}}
        \fontsize{10}{12}
        \label{fig:GRU_cell}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}
The four equations in the GRU cell:
\begin{align}
    % \label{eq:gru_cell}
    % \begin{split}
        r_t &=  \sigma (W_r h_{t-1} + U_r x_t + b_r) \label{eq:reset_gate} \\
        z_t &= \sigma (W_z h_{t-1} + U_z x_t + b_z) \label{eq:update_gate} \\
        \hat{h_t} &= \phi(W (r_t \circ h_{t-1}) + U x_t + b) \label{eq:h_hat} \\
        h_t &= z_t \circ h_{t-1} + (1-z_t) \circ \hat{s_t} \label{eq:h_out}
    % \end{split}
\end{align}
And the two activation functions:
\begin{align}
    \sigma (x) &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} \label{eq:sigmoid} \\
    \phi (x) &= \frac{ e^{x}-e^{-x} }{ e^{x}+e^{-x} } \label{eq:tanh}
\end{align}

Where $r_t$ is the \textbf{Rest Gate} or \textbf{Reset Gate} and $z_t$ is the \textbf{Update Gate}.
Notation $\circ$ means elementwise multiplication for two tensors of same shape.
Operator $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function (Equation \ref{eq:sigmoid}), which maps the input value to (0,1).
$\phi$ is the hyperbolic tangent (tanh) function (Equation \ref{eq:tanh}), mapping the input value to (-1,1).
At each timestep, a single GRU cell can take in two tensors, one is the current input tensor $x_t$ and another is the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, which is the output from last timestep.
In the process of calculating each gate value (Equation \ref{eq:reset_gate}, \ref{eq:update_gate}) and $\hat{h_t}$ (Equation \ref{eq:h_hat}),
hidden state from last timestep ($h_{t-1}$) will be multiplied by unique weights ($W_r$, $W_z$, $W$),
and so does the input tensor ($x_t$) will be multiplied by its own weights ($U_r$, $U_z$, $U$).
After the multiplication, 3 biases ($b_r$, $b_z$, $b$) will be added to the results before entering the activation functions.

A hyperparameter named \textbf{hidden dimension} or \textbf{number of hidden units} specified the dimensions for all hidden states, which is a vector.
This hyperparameter also determine the number of each gates, i.e. with the setting of $u$ hidden units,
the shape of all hidden states should be $1 \times u$ and there will be $u$ reset gates, $u$ update gates in each cell.
Typically with the larger the hidden dimension, the more complex the model will be.
However, sophisticated models tend to overfit.

Figure \ref{fig:two_layer_GRU} demonstrated how hidden states are passed from one layer to another.
Where $n$ is the order of our input, representing how many the time bins to be fed into the model at a time for decoding the current kinematic states.
The input data at a time contains the firing rate at this moment $x_t$ and the past firing rate from $x_{t-n+1}$ to $x_{t-1}$.
The corrosponding outputs of our model are the kinematic variables at time $t$.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{two_layer_GRU.png}
        \caption{Architecture of one-way two-layer GRU}
        \fontsize{10}{12}
        \label{fig:two_layer_GRU}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}
In the first GRU layer, $n$ bins of firing rate are fed chronologically.
Hidden state $h^{j}_{i}$ denotes the output tensor from the cell in timestep $i$ and layer $j$, and will be the input to the cell in timestep $i+1$ and layer $j+1$.
The size of $h^{j}_{i}$ should be $1 \times u$ since $u$ is a predefined parameter.
Ideally tensor $h^{2}_{t}$ should contain the information from the firing rate $x_{t-n+1}$ to $x_{t-1}$, thus by feeding it to some fully connect layers we can acquire the desired output.

The so called bidirectional RNN is made by simply adding another feeding of the same input sequence in reverse order.
As in Figure \ref{fig:Bi_two_layer_GRU}, but the size of the hidden state $h^{j}_{i}$ will be $1 \times 2u$, 
the first half of the this vector contains the information by feeding input sequence forward the last half by feeding backward.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{two-way_two-layer_GRU.png}
        \caption{Architecture of Bidirectional Two-layer GRU}
        \fontsize{10}{12}
        \label{fig:Bi_two_layer_GRU}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}
It is up to the internal dynamics of the GRU to determine the degree of forget and keep the previous information.
